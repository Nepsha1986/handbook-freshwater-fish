## Overview

_Otocinclus vittatus_ is a miniature member of the armoured catfish family (Loricariidae), inhabiting Amazon tributaries
and the Paraná basin across Brazil, Peru, Bolivia and Paraguay. In the wild the fish gather in large shoals in brightly
lit shallows, grazing biofilm and algal growth from stones, driftwood and the leaves of marginal vegetation.

Adults reach just 4-5 cm and live 3-5 years. The body is elongated and covered in bony plates, with a broad dark stripe
running along the flank from snout to tail base, a grey-olive back and a pale belly. The sucker mouth sits underneath
and is adapted exclusively to scraping growth from hard surfaces. Females can be told by a broader, rounder body viewed
from above, though the difference only shows in adults.

The Otocinclus is probably the most effective and at the same time the safest algae eater for a planted tank: unlike
many catfish it damages not even delicate leaves, and it leaves fry and shrimp alone. There is, however, an important
caveat behind its reputation as a "difficult" species: the overwhelming majority of Otocinclus in the trade are
wild-caught fish that have been through long transport and starvation. Losses in the first two to three weeks after
purchase are very high, and they almost always stem from emaciation rather than from mistakes in husbandry.

Care level runs from easy to intermediate: the fish is undemanding about water parameters but demanding about their
stability and about the availability of food.

## Tank Requirements

The minimum is 60 litres, and it is worth understanding this correctly: the constraint is not volume but grazing area.
In a freshly set up tank there is simply nothing for Otocinclus to eat. Introduce them no earlier than two to three
months after start-up, once a stable biofilm has established on the glass, stones and driftwood.

Keep the species in a group of at least six: singly they hide, lose their appetite and live markedly shorter lives. In a
shoal they graze openly and actively throughout the day.

Water parameters: temperature 22-26 °C, pH 6.0-7.5, general hardness (GH) 6-15 dGH. The range is wide, but the species
is extremely sensitive to nitrogenous compounds: traces of ammonia that other fish shrug off can be lethal to an
Otocinclus.

For the layout, use a densely planted tank with driftwood and stones offering the maximum grazing surface. Broad-leaved
plants such as swords and anubias are especially valuable, as the fish spend most of their time on them. Keep the
substrate soft, preferably sand. Flow should be moderate but the water well oxygenated — Otocinclus are among the first
to react to a shortfall by rising to the surface.

## Feeding and Diet

_O. vittatus_ is a narrowly specialised herbivore living on diatoms, biofilm and soft algal growth. This is the source
of the most common misconception: that the catfish will "feed themselves". In a mature tank that holds true for a while,
but a group of six to eight strips the available growth within a couple of weeks and then begins to starve slowly.

Supplementary feeding must be constant, not an afterthought. The staple is algae wafers for herbivorous catfish plus
blanched vegetables: courgette, cucumber, pumpkin, spinach and lettuce leaves. Slice the vegetables, weight them with a
stone or clip them to a holder, leave them in for 12-24 hours and then remove what is left.

Feed in the evening after lights out, when quicker tank mates have settled. The sign of an underfed fish is a sunken,
drawn-in belly seen in profile; a well-fed Otocinclus has a distinctly rounded abdomen. This is the most reliable check
available and worth making regularly.

Animal foods are neither needed nor meaningfully digested by this species.

## Care and Maintenance

Change 20-30% of the water weekly, always pre-conditioned and temperature-matched. Ammonia and nitrite should read a
steady zero, nitrate no higher than 20 mg/l.

Chemistry deserves particular attention. Otocinclus are acutely sensitive to copper-based preparations, which feature in
many treatments for algae, snails and external parasites: a dose that is safe for other fish is lethal to them. The same
applies to most algicides — they must not be used in a tank housing Otocinclus.

Do not scrub the tank spotless. Leave the growth on the back wall, the stones and the driftwood: that is the fish's food
base. Rinse filter media in old tank water every three to four weeks.

The first three weeks after purchase are the critical period. Keep new fish in a quarantine tank rich in algal growth
and soft food, watching the fullness of the belly. The main problems in this window are emaciation and bacterial
infections in weakened individuals. Fish that settle in prove very hardy thereafter.

## Compatibility

_O. vittatus_ is utterly peaceful and safe for everything, fry, shrimp and the most delicate plants included. Choosing
tank mates comes down to making sure nobody harms the catfish itself.

Excellent companions are small, calm species: [Hengel's Rasbora](/en/trigonostigma-hengeli),
[Harlequin Rasbora](/en/trigonostigma-heteromorpha), [Green Neon Tetra](/en/paracheirodon-simulans),
[Ember Tetra](/en/hyphessobrycon-amandae), [Celestial Pearl Danio](/en/danio-margaritatus) and
[Pygmy Corydoras](/en/corydoras-pygmaeus). Among dwarf cichlids the [Ram Cichlid](/en/mikrogeophagus-ramirezi) and
[Borelli's Dwarf Cichlid](/en/apistogramma-borellii) work well. In a shrimp tank the Otocinclus is one of the best
choices available.

Pairing with the [Siamese Algae Eater](/en/crossocheilus-siamensis) also works nicely: the two eat different types of
algae and do not compete directly.

Unsuitable: large and aggressive fish, and any medium-sized cichlid; boisterous species that intercept food before it
reaches the bottom; and large suckermouth catfish such as the [Sailfin Pleco](/en/pterygoplichthys-gibbiceps), which
will push a small fish off the feeding surfaces.

## Breeding

Breeding _Otocinclus_ under home conditions succeeds rarely and counts as a difficult undertaking. The main reason is
that aquarium stock consists almost entirely of wild-caught fish, and the cues that trigger spawning are hard to
reproduce in captivity.

Spawnings do happen nonetheless, usually spontaneously, in established densely planted tanks holding a large group of
well-fed fish. The trigger is most often a series of water changes with cooler, softer water, imitating the rainy
season, combined with a generous vegetable diet.

The female lays eggs in small batches of five to ten on the glass, broad leaves and driftwood, up to 50-70 eggs per
spawning in total. The species shows no parental care, and in a community tank the clutch is usually eaten, so to raise
fry the eggs are transferred gently, still attached to their leaf, into a separate container filled with water from the
main tank and given gentle aeration.

Incubation takes two to three days. The fry are minute and from the first days eat exactly what the adults do: biofilm
and soft algae. This is precisely why the grow-out container is prepared in advance, with stones and glass already
covered in growth. Crushed algae wafers and liquid fry foods work well as a supplement. Growth is slow: the young reach
2 cm at around two months.
