## Overview

The Pale Chub (_Zacco platypus_) is a riverine shoaling cyprinid from the fast rivers and mountain streams of Japan,
Korea, China and the Russian Far East. Modern sources often list it as _Opsariichthys platypus_, so do not be surprised
to meet both names — they refer to the same fish.

This is not a typical tropical aquarium fish but a genuine inhabitant of cold, fast water. The body is elongated and
torpedo-shaped, with large scales and a powerful caudal peduncle — the build of a fish that spends its life in the
current. Adults reach 12-15 cm and live 3-5 years. They are active by day and hold station behind rocks near the bottom
at night.

Outside the breeding season pale chubs look plain: a silvery body with a bluish sheen and a row of diffuse vertical
blotches. In spring, though, the males transform — blue-green and violet bars spread across the body, the fins flush
orange-red, and pearly breeding tubercles erupt on the head and gill covers. That transformation is the reason the
species is kept at all.

Be clear about what you are taking on: the pale chub suits a dedicated aquarist willing to build a cold-water river
tank, not a standard tropical community. The fish itself is hardy and undemanding, but its requirements for current,
oxygen and temperature differ sharply from the usual.

## Tank Requirements

The formal minimum is 80 litres, but that figure fits juveniles or a pair of adults. For a proper shoal of six to eight
fish 12-15 cm long I recommend 200 litres or more, at least 120 cm long. Length matters more than volume here: pale
chubs are marathon swimmers and need a run-up.

Optimal water parameters:

- **Temperature:** 18-24°C. A heater is usually unnecessary; in summer the opposite is true and cooling may be needed —
  above 26°C the fish begin to suffocate. A winter drop to 14-16°C is taken in stride and is in fact needed to condition
  them for spawning.
- **pH:** 6.0-7.5, with neutral water the optimum.
- **Hardness (GH):** 5-15 — soft to moderately hard, as in a mountain river.

Model the layout on a river bed: sand and rounded gravel, larger stones, a few pieces of driftwood. Plants are optional
and hold poorly in strong current — use anubias, ferns and mosses tied to rocks. Open swimming space matters more than
decoration.

Current and oxygen are the crux. You need a powerful filter (eight to ten tank volumes per hour) plus an additional flow
pump aimed along the long wall. Cold, oxygen-rich, fast-moving water is the single most important condition for success
with this species.

Last but very much not least: pale chubs are outstanding jumpers. A tight-fitting lid with no gaps is mandatory. An open
tank with these fish will cost you losses, guaranteed.

## Feeding and Diet

In the wild pale chubs actively hunt drifting food: falling insects, caddisfly and mayfly larvae, small crustaceans,
plus algae scraped off stones.

In the aquarium they take anything on offer: quality pellets and flakes for cold-water fish, live and frozen brine
shrimp, daphnia, glassworms and bloodworms. Larger live food — insects, small crickets, earthworms — draws a
particularly enthusiastic response. A spirulina-based plant supplement once a week does no harm.

Feed adults once or twice a day and juveniles three times. Drop the food into the current: pale chubs are built to catch
it in midwater and barely forage off the bottom. Anything that lands on the substrate will stay there and rot, so factor
that into your portions.

Their appetite is excellent and overfeeding is easy. In warmer water (above 24°C) metabolism speeds up while dissolved
oxygen drops — cut portions back during hot spells.

## Care and Maintenance

The main work with this species is keeping the water clean and well oxygenated.

Change 25-30% weekly: pale chubs are active, eat a lot and put a real load on the filter. Match the temperature of the
fresh water — a cool water change is fine, a warm one is stressful. Vacuum the substrate weekly and rinse filter media
in tank water you have just siphoned out.

Watch the temperature in summer. Overheating is the most common cause of death for this species in captivity. As you
approach 26°C, increase aeration, aim a pump at the surface, swap the lid for mesh, and use a fan or an aquarium chiller
if needed.

Parameter checks are standard: ammonia and nitrite at zero, nitrate below 40 mg/l. Biological filtration works more
slowly in cold water, so start a pale chub tank with generous filter capacity and stock it gradually.

Keep them in a shoal of six to eight or more. Alone or in small numbers they are skittish, dash about and injure
themselves on the glass; in a proper shoal they are calm and confident. There is a clear hierarchy and males test each
other from time to time, but injuries are rare.

## Compatibility

Pale chubs are not aggressive, but they are extremely fast and active: slower tank mates simply go hungry next to them.
Pick company by temperature and temperament at the same time.

Good tank mates:

- [white cloud mountain minnow](/en/tanichthys-albonubes) — cool water and shoaling habits, though only in a
  sufficiently large tank;
- [zebra danio](/en/danio-rerio) — the same 18-24°C range and comparable energy;
- [weather loach](/en/misgurnus-anguillicaudatus) — a calm cold-water bottom dweller;
- subtropical loaches such as [Acanthocobitis zonalternans](/en/acanthocobitis-zonalternans) — they occupy the lower
  level and do not compete for food in midwater.

Poor choices: any warmth-loving tropical species needing 26°C and up; slow, long-finned fish including fancy
[goldfish](/en/carassius-auratus) varieties, which cannot reach the food in time and suffer in constant current; small
fish and shrimp, which an adult pale chub treats as prey; and territorial cichlids.

The species is at its easiest, and looks its best, in a species-only cold-water tank styled after a mountain river.

## Breeding

Breeding the pale chub is harder than breeding livebearers but entirely achievable: the key is reproducing the seasonal
cycle.

Through autumn and winter hold the fish for two to three months at 14-16°C with a shortened photoperiod and moderate
feeding. Then raise the temperature gradually to 20-22°C over two to three weeks, extend the day length and switch the
breeders to plenty of live food. It is exactly this swing that ripens the gonads; without a winter rest spawning
generally does not happen.

Set up the spawning tank with a layer of coarse gravel on the bottom, strong current and good aeration. Ready males take
on their vivid breeding colours and pearly tubercles, and females fill out noticeably. Use one female to two males. The
eggs are scattered over the gravel and fall between the stones; some fish will deposit them on spawning mops instead.

Remove the breeders immediately after spawning — they eat eggs readily. Larvae hatch in two to three days depending on
temperature, live on the yolk sac for another three or four, then move on to infusoria, rotifers and "green water". From
about a week old they take baby brine shrimp. The fry grow unevenly, so grade them by size regularly or the largest will
outcompete the rest.
