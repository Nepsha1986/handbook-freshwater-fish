## Overview

_Lamprologus ocellatus_ is endemic to Lake Tanganyika and one of the most characteristic members of the shell-dwelling
cichlid group. In the wild it lives on sandy shelves at depths of 5-20 metres, where the bottom is strewn with the empty
shells of the gastropod _Neothauma_. Each fish claims a single shell, and its entire life — feeding, resting, spawning,
defence — revolves around that refuge.

Adults measure just 4-5 cm, females smaller still at around 3-3.5 cm, and live 5-8 years. The body is compact and sandy
beige with a pearlescent sheen, the eyes disproportionately large, and the gill cover carries a dark ocellus ringed in
gold that gave the species its name, _ocellatus_. The dorsal and anal fins are edged in white or pale blue, noticeably
brighter in males.

Behaviour is the main reason for the species' popularity. The Shell Dweller buries its shell in the sand at just the
right angle, digs it out again, drags it to a new spot and defends it furiously against anything that comes within 10-15
cm — fish three times its size and the aquarist's fingers included. Beyond its own patch, however, it bothers nobody.

Care level is intermediate, arising not from fussiness but from strict demands on mineralisation: this is a rift lake
species that needs hard alkaline water.

## Tank Requirements

The formal minimum for a pair is 40 litres, and this is that rare case where cichlids genuinely suit a small aquarium.
Far more important than volume is floor area: the fish divide that, so the tank should be long and low. A colony of five
or six needs 100 litres and upwards at 80-100 cm long.

Water parameters: temperature 24-26 °C, pH 7.8-9.0, general hardness (GH) 10-20 dGH. Soft water is contraindicated; if
necessary, mineralise it with a commercial Tanganyika salt mix. A sharp drop in pH is poorly tolerated.

The key element of the layout is sand at least 5-7 cm deep. This is what the fish dig in, and on a thin layer they
cannot live normally. The second essential is the shells: at least two or three per fish, so they have something to
choose between and somewhere to relocate when conflicts arise. Achatina snail shells work well, as does anything of
similar size with an opening roughly matching the fish's diameter.

Keep the rest of the decor minimal: a couple of flat stones to divide the space, and open ground. Plants are unnecessary
and barely grow in this water — if you want them, limit yourself to anubias or vallisneria anchored outside the digging
zone. Filtration should be moderate and the flow gentle, or the fish will spend their time re-burying their shells.

## Feeding and Diet

In nature _L. ocellatus_ is a micropredator, picking small crustaceans, insect larvae and zooplankton out of the sand
and the water column. It takes practically no plant matter.

The aquarium staple should be quality small cichlid pellets high in animal protein. Two or three times a week offer live
and frozen foods: brine shrimp, cyclops, daphnia, mysis, small bloodworms. It is on this diet that the fish develop full
colour and spawn readily.

Strictly avoid tubifex and foods made from warm-blooded animals — Tanganyikan cichlids handle fatty food badly and
easily develop intestinal inflammation.

Feed twice a day in portions consumed within a minute. Bear in mind that the fish barely leaves the vicinity of its
shell and will not chase food around the tank: the food has to sink to the bottom near it.

## Care and Maintenance

A weekly 20-25% water change is the standard routine. Always bring the replacement water up to the tank's hardness and
pH: for rift lake species a swing towards soft water is more dangerous than a modest build-up of nitrate.

Vacuum only the top layer of sand in open areas and leave the shell zone alone — the fish will spend days rebuilding
wrecked burrows, and during spawning it can cost the clutch. Rinse filter media in old tank water every three to four
weeks. Keep nitrate below 25 mg/l.

Check pH weekly: in a tank without buffering substrate it drifts down steadily as organics accumulate. Crushed coral or
aragonite sand in the filter is a good solution, stabilising alkalinity.

A healthy Shell Dweller is permanently busy: digging, shifting grains of sand, peering out of its shell and shooting out
the instant food appears. Cause for concern: sitting in the shelter for long stretches, fading colour, refusing food and
a swollen belly. The main problems are ich and digestive upsets from unsuitable food. Quarantine new arrivals for two to
three weeks.

## Compatibility

_L. ocellatus_ is territorial, but the radius of its claims is measured in centimetres and it is far too small to do
real harm. The problem is usually the reverse: the neighbours get in its way.

The best arrangement is a species tank with a colony of several fish. If you want company, choose Tanganyikan species
that occupy other levels: [Caudopunctatus Cichlid](/en/neolamprologus-caudopunctatus),
[Princess of Burundi](/en/neolamprologus-brichardi), [Lemon Cichlid](/en/neolamprologus-leleupi),
[Yellowfin Xenotilapia](/en/xenotilapia-flavipinnis), and the [Dwarf Petricola Catfish](/en/synodontis-petricola) as a
bottom dweller. Another shell dweller, the [Multi Shell Dweller](/en/neolamprologus-multifasciatus), only works in a
tank long enough for two independent colonies.

Unsuitable: large aggressive cichlids from Tanganyika and Malawi, the [Auratus Cichlid](/en/melanochromis-auratus) and
[Zebra Mbuna](/en/pseudotropheus-zebra) among them — they will simply displace a small fish; any species that digs and
wrecks the shells; and anything needing soft acidic water, as the biotopes cannot be reconciled.

Shrimp and small snails do not last long in such a tank: both are treated as food.

## Breeding

Breeding _L. ocellatus_ is straightforward and usually happens by itself once conditions are right. No dedicated
breeding tank is needed — the species reproduces perfectly well in a species aquarium.

The simplest route to a pair is to stock one male and two or three females: the species is polygamous, and in the wild a
male controls a patch containing several female shells. The male is easy to identify by size, being half again as large.
Optimal spawning conditions: temperature 26 °C, pH 8.0-8.5, and generous live food for one to two weeks beforehand.

The female lays 20-40 eggs inside the shell, on its inner wall, and barely leaves the shelter, fanning the clutch with
her fins. The male guards the territory outside. Incubation takes two to three days, and the larvae remain in the shell
for roughly another week.

You will not spot the free-swimming fry straight away — at first they hug the shell's mouth and vanish inside at the
slightest alarm. They can take brine shrimp nauplii and microworms immediately. There is no need to remove the parents:
the young grow up alongside them, and in time some of the juveniles claim shells of their own. The problem arrives
later, when the tank runs out of room — at that point move the grown fish out, or serious territorial fights begin.
