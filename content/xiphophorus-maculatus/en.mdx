## Overview

The Platy (_Xiphophorus maculatus_), together with the [guppy](/en/poecilia-reticulata) and the
[swordtail](/en/xiphophorus-hellerii), makes up the classic trio of livebearers that almost every community tank starts
with. The species comes from slow rivers, ditches and coastal marshes on the Atlantic slope of Central America, from
Veracruz in Mexico down to Belize and Guatemala. Unlike the swordtail, the platy is a fish of still and slow-moving,
heavily vegetated water.

The body is short and stocky with a rounded caudal fin — a "float" next to the elongated swordtails. Adults reach 5-7
cm, with females larger and noticeably fuller than males. Sexing is easy: the male's anal fin is modified into a narrow
gonopodium, while the female's stays broad and fan-shaped. Lifespan is 3-5 years.

The wild form is plain — a greyish body with dark spots at the base of the tail — but it has given rise to hundreds of
cultivated varieties: red, yellow, "tuxedo", Mickey Mouse (with its telltale tail marking), spotted, calico and neon
blue. Veiltail and high-fin forms exist too, but as usual they are weaker and fussier about water quality.

One more practical detail: the platy hybridises freely with the swordtail and with the
[variatus platy](/en/xiphophorus-variatus). Most of the vivid shop varieties are the product of exactly such crosses,
which is why offspring in a community tank often turn out mixed and unpredictable.

## Tank Requirements

The minimum volume is 40 litres for a small group of five or six fish. This is one of the few aquarium fish for which a
modest tank genuinely suffices: platies are small, calm and do not need long swimming lanes. Still, the bigger the tank,
the more stable the parameters and the better the fish look.

Optimal water parameters:

- **Temperature:** 22-28°C, ideally 23-25°C. Many keepers run platies without a heater in a warm room — the species
  allows it, but temperature swings do not do them any favours.
- **pH:** 6.8-8.0. Neutral to slightly alkaline water is preferable.
- **Hardness (GH):** 10-30. The platy is a hard-water fish; in soft water it fades, weakens and breeds poorly.

The layout is simple: dark substrate, dense planting around the edges (vallisneria, cryptocoryne, hornwort, java moss)
and open space in the middle. Floating plants are essential if you expect fry to appear on their own — their roots are
exactly where the young hide.

Any filter rated at three to four tank volumes per hour will do; platies dislike strong current and will avoid it. A lid
is advisable — they jump less often than swordtails, but it does happen.

Hardness deserves a separate note. If your tap water is soft (GH below 8), add a little crushed coral or marble gravel
to the filter — a simple, reliable way to raise GH and stabilise pH.

## Feeding and Diet

Platies are omnivores with a strong bias towards plant matter: in the wild they spend most of their time grazing algae
and detritus. That is why an all-protein diet harms them more than most fish — it leads to obesity and a bloated gut.

Build the diet around quality flakes or small livebearer pellets containing spirulina and other plant ingredients.
Supplement two or three times a week with live or frozen food: brine shrimp, daphnia, cyclops, small bloodworms. Once a
week offer blanched vegetables — courgette, spinach, lettuce leaves.

Feed once or twice a day in portions eaten within a minute or two. The platy's small mouth cannot handle large pellets,
so choose an appropriate size or crush the food. A weekly fasting day does them good.

Like swordtails, platies willingly graze soft algae off glass, driftwood and leaves — a small but welcome contribution
to tank maintenance.

## Care and Maintenance

The platy is a beginner's fish, and keeping it asks for nothing beyond basic discipline.

Change 20-25% of the water weekly, matching temperature and hardness. Vacuum the substrate at least every other week.
Rinse filter media in tank water you have just siphoned out, never under the tap.

Test the water every one to two weeks: ammonia and nitrite at zero, nitrate below 40 mg/l. Platies are fairly tough, but
chronically high nitrate combined with soft water is a direct route to disease.

The most common problem in this species is so-called wasting and bloat: the fish grows thin despite a normal appetite,
or conversely swells up and hangs at the surface. In the great majority of cases the root cause is not an infection but
a monotonous dry-food diet and water that is too soft. Among genuine infections, ich is the usual one, typically showing
up after the fish are chilled during a water change.

Keep them in a group of five or six with females in the majority — two or three females per male. Male platies are not
as pushy as swordtails, but with too few females they will wear them out with constant courtship.

## Compatibility

The platy is one of the most peaceful aquarium fish there is: no aggression, no fin-nipping. The main criterion when
choosing tank mates is matching water parameters, not temperament.

Good tank mates:

- [swordtail](/en/xiphophorus-hellerii) and [variatus platy](/en/xiphophorus-variatus) — closest relatives with the same
  requirements (with the hybridisation caveat above);
- [molly](/en/poecilia-sphenops) and [guppy](/en/poecilia-reticulata) — hard water and a gentle disposition;
- [zebra danio](/en/danio-rerio) — active, hardy and mostly in the upper layer;
- [bronze corydoras](/en/corydoras-aeneus) — a bottom-level cleaner;
- [otocinclus](/en/otocinclus-vittatus) — a tiny algae eater that bothers nobody.

Poor choices: the [tiger barb](/en/puntius-tetrazona) and other fin-nippers — veiltail platies suffer from them first.
Large cichlids treat platies as food. Soft-water characins such as the [neon tetra](/en/paracheirodon-innesi) get along
temperamentally but need completely different water, so keeping them together means a compromise that suits neither
species.

## Breeding

The platy is a livebearer, and breeding here happens by itself rather than requiring effort. Put a male and a couple of
females in a tank and within a few weeks you will find fry among the plants.

Gestation lasts 4-6 weeks and ends with 20-60 fully formed fry — up to 80 from a large mature female. An approaching
birth shows in a swollen, angular belly and a darkened gravid spot near the anal fin. Like all poeciliids, the female
can store sperm and produce another two or three broods without mating again — a female bought at a shop is often
already pregnant.

No special setup is needed, but if you want to save the brood there are two routes. Plant the tank thickly with moss and
floating plants and simply let some of the fry survive on their own (usually 10-20% make it). Or move the female to a
separate 10-20 litre container full of plants and return her right after she gives birth; breeding traps work as well,
but only for short spells, since the confinement can trigger premature birth.

Feed the fry four to five times a day: crushed flakes, baby brine shrimp, microworms. They grow quickly — sex becomes
visible at around two months and maturity arrives at three to four. That is exactly why platy numbers snowball in a
community tank: plan where the offspring will go before the first brood arrives.
