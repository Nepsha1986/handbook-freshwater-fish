## Overview

_Neolamprologus pulcher_ is endemic to Lake Tanganyika, living along rocky shorelines at depths of 5-45 metres.
Aquarists know it under the trade names "Daffodil" and "Princess of Zambia"; several geographic forms are available,
differing in the intensity of their yellow. Its closest relative is the
[Princess of Burundi](/en/neolamprologus-brichardi), long considered the same species and one it hybridises with freely
in the aquarium.

Adults reach 7-10 cm and live 8-10 years. The body is elongated and greyish-beige with a soft yellow wash along the
flanks and blue sparks in the unpaired fins. The tail is lyre-shaped with extended upper and lower rays, and the gill
cover carries a distinctive dark marking edged in blue. Sexing by colour is nearly impossible: males are only slightly
larger with marginally longer fin extensions.

What the species is really kept for is its social behaviour. _N. pulcher_ lives not in pairs but in family clans: grown
fry from previous broods stay alongside the breeding pair, forgo nests of their own and help the parents instead —
guarding the territory, cleaning the clutch, clearing the entrance to the shelter. It is one of the few documented cases
of cooperative breeding in fish, and it plays out fully in the aquarium provided the juveniles are left in place.

Care level is intermediate. The fish is undemanding about food and hardy, but it requires hard alkaline water and
adequate floor area.

## Tank Requirements

The minimum is 100 litres for a pair, but a clan of six to ten — which is how the species comes into its own — needs 200
litres and upwards at no less than 100 cm long. As with most Tanganyikan cichlids, floor area matters, not height.

Water parameters: temperature 24-26 °C, pH 7.8-9.0, general hardness (GH) 10-20 dGH. The species handles acidification
badly, and a gradual downward drift in pH is the most common problem in these tanks.

Build the layout around stone. Assemble piles of flat rocks and slabs with caves, crevices and tunnels, resting them
squarely on the tank floor rather than on the substrate: these fish dig energetically, and a structure set on a sand
cushion will subside in time. Use sand 3-5 cm deep, and leave open swimming areas between the rock groups.

Plants are optional, but vallisneria and anubias grow well enough in hard water — anchor them outside the digging zone.
Filtration should be strong, turning over five to six tank volumes per hour: the species likes clean, well-oxygenated
water and moderate flow. Crushed coral in the filter is a convenient way to stabilise alkalinity.

## Feeding and Diet

In nature _N. pulcher_ feeds on zooplankton taken from the water column and on small invertebrates picked off the rock
surfaces. It is a carnivore, but with no appetite for large prey.

The aquarium staple should be quality pellets and flakes for medium-sized cichlids. Two or three times a week add live
and frozen foods: brine shrimp, cyclops, mysis, daphnia, small bloodworms. A small spirulina-based supplement once a
week aids digestion.

As with all Tanganyikan cichlids, tubifex and foods made from warm-blooded animals are excluded entirely: fatty food
provokes intestinal inflammation, which runs a severe course in this group.

Feed adults twice a day in portions consumed within a couple of minutes. The clan feeds together and fast, but make sure
the food reaches the juveniles too, not only the dominant pair.

## Care and Maintenance

A weekly 20-25% water change is mandatory. Prepare the water in advance and match it for hardness and pH: this species
tolerates a swing towards soft water worse than a moderate build-up of nitrate.

Check pH weekly — in a tank with accumulating organics it creeps steadily downward, and a fall below 7.5 shows in the
fish immediately. Keep nitrate below 25 mg/l, ammonia and nitrite at zero. Rinse filter media in old tank water every
three to four weeks.

Vacuum the sand in open areas without destroying burrows and the excavations under rocks. Check the stability of the
stonework periodically: the fish undermine the bases, and heavy rockwork can eventually collapse.

A healthy pulcher is active, holds station in the middle layer near its rock, keeps its fins spread and shows a crisp
gill-cover marking. Warning signals are hanging in a corner, fading colour, refusing food and whitish stringy faeces.
The main diseases are ich and intestinal infections from an unsuitable diet; quarantine new arrivals for two to three
weeks.

## Compatibility

_N. pulcher_ is territorial, but its aggression is moderate and directed mainly at its own kind and at anything
intruding on the nesting zone. Outside of spawning it is a perfectly sociable fish.

Good companions are Tanganyikan species occupying other levels and other kinds of shelter:
[Caudopunctatus Cichlid](/en/neolamprologus-caudopunctatus), [Lemon Cichlid](/en/neolamprologus-leleupi),
[Cylindricus Cichlid](/en/neolamprologus-cylindricus), [Compressiceps Cichlid](/en/altolamprologus-compressiceps) and
[Yellowfin Xenotilapia](/en/xenotilapia-flavipinnis). On the bottom level the
[Dwarf Petricola Catfish](/en/synodontis-petricola) and [Cuckoo Catfish](/en/synodontis-multipunctatus) fit well.

A word on the [Princess of Burundi](/en/neolamprologus-brichardi): the two are so closely related that they produce
hybrid offspring freely. Keep them together only if breeding does not interest you.

Unsuitable: [shell dwellers](/en/lamprologus-ocellatus) in a small tank, which will be pushed off the sandy patches;
large aggressive Malawians such as the [Auratus Cichlid](/en/melanochromis-auratus); and any species needing soft acidic
water.

## Breeding

Breeding _N. pulcher_ is simple and needs no separate breeding tank — indeed, removing the fish is not merely
unnecessary but counterproductive: all the species' interesting behaviour emerges only in the intact group.

Do not hand-pick a pair. Take six to eight juveniles, grow them on together and wait for a dominant pair to form; the
rest will remain in the clan as helpers. Sexual maturity comes at eight to twelve months.

Spawning conditions: temperature 25-26 °C, pH 8.0-8.5, stable parameters and a suitable shelter — a cave, a crevice
between rocks or a ceramic tube. The female lays 50-200 eggs on the ceiling or wall of the shelter. Incubation takes two
to three days, and the larvae stay in the cave for roughly another week.

Then comes the interesting part. The parents shepherd the brood near the nest while helpers from earlier generations
patrol the territory's borders and see off intruders — in the aquarium you can often watch a juvenile drive a larger
fish away from the shelter entrance. Fry take brine shrimp nauplii and microworms from the first days and grow up
comfortably alongside the adults.

There is no need to move the young out until the clan outgrows the tank. The sign that it is time is a rise in serious
scuffles and torn fins among the juveniles: it means no free territory is left.
