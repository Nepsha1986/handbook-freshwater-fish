## Overview

The Swordtail (_Xiphophorus hellerii_) is one of those fish nearly every aquarist starts out with. This livebearer from
the family Poeciliidae comes from Central America, ranging from southern Mexico through Guatemala to Honduras. In the
wild it lives in fast streams, spring-fed rivers and densely planted channels with clean, hard water — worth remembering
when you set up water parameters at home.

The species' signature feature is the elongated lower ray of the male's caudal fin, the "sword". It is not a weapon and
plays no part in fights: it is purely a courtship ornament that females use to judge a partner. The body is elongated
and sturdy with a deep caudal peduncle — the build of a strong swimmer. Adults reach 10-14 cm including the male's
sword; females are noticeably heavier-bodied but lack the extension. Lifespan is 3-5 years with good care.

The wild form is olive-green with a red lateral stripe, though it is rarely offered for sale. Breeders have produced
dozens of variants: red, black, calico, "pineapple", koi, plus veiltail and lyretail forms. Keep one thing in mind: the
more elaborate the strain, the weaker the fish — veiltail swordtails swim poorly and suffer from fin rot more often. For
beginners I always recommend the plain red or green forms.

The species is also known for a curious trait: females can change sex. A mature female kept without a male for a long
time may develop a gonopodium and a short sword, becoming a functional male. It is less common than aquarium lore
suggests, but it does happen.

## Tank Requirements

The minimum volume for a group of swordtails is 75 litres. That is not a formality: these fish are constantly on the
move through the middle and upper layers, and in cramped quarters the males start settling scores. A tank 60 cm or
longer with wide open swimming space suits them far better than a tall cube.

Optimal water parameters:

- **Temperature:** 22-28°C. The fish are most comfortable at 24-26°C. The top of the range speeds up metabolism and
  noticeably shortens their lives.
- **pH:** 7.0-8.2. Swordtails are hard-water fish and tolerate acidic conditions poorly.
- **Hardness (GH):** 10-25. In water that is too soft the fish lose colour and females struggle to carry their broods.

A dark substrate is the better choice — it makes the fish's colours look far richer. Plant in groups along the back and
side panels: vallisneria, cryptocoryne, elodea, hornwort. Floating plants (water lettuce, frogbit) diffuse the light and
give fry somewhere to hide. Always leave the centre of the tank open.

A lid is mandatory. Swordtails are excellent jumpers, and losing a fish that leapt out of an open tank overnight is the
most frustrating — and most common — beginner's mistake.

Filtration should be moderate to strong; the fish enjoy a gentle current and will play in the outflow. Aeration is
advisable, especially in hot weather and in well-stocked tanks.

## Feeding and Diet

In the wild swordtails are omnivores: they graze biofilm off rocks, pick up fallen insects and hunt small invertebrates.
Plant matter makes up a large share of their diet and should not be ignored — an all-protein menu leads to obesity and
digestive problems.

Build the diet around quality flakes or small livebearer pellets with spirulina. Two or three times a week add live or
frozen food: brine shrimp, daphnia, cyclops, small bloodworms. Once a week offer blanched vegetables — courgette,
spinach, a slice of cucumber — clipped to a suction feeder.

Feed adults once or twice a day in portions they finish within a couple of minutes. A weekly fasting day does them good.
Remember that swordtails will beg constantly, but overfeeding and leftovers rotting in the substrate cause far more
trouble than underfeeding.

A useful bonus: swordtails willingly graze hair algae and soft biofilm off the glass and decorations, acting as modest
but genuine clean-up crew.

## Care and Maintenance

The swordtail is an "easy" fish, and its care comes down to being consistent about small things.

Change 20-25% of the water weekly, matching temperature and hardness. A sudden drop in GH during a water change is
tolerated worse than a small temperature swing. Vacuum the substrate weekly: food trapped in the gravel is the main
source of nitrate.

Rinse the filter media in tank water you have just siphoned out, never under the tap — that keeps the nitrifying
bacteria alive. Testing ammonia, nitrite and nitrate every one to two weeks is enough; in an established tank ammonia
and nitrite should read zero and nitrate stay below 40 mg/l.

Watch the fish at feeding time — that is the best moment to spot a problem. Warning signs are clamped fins, hanging at
the surface, refusing food, and white grains on the body (ich). Swordtails are robust overall but sensitive to
accumulated nitrate and temperature swings: disease in a swordtail tank is usually the result of skipped water changes
rather than an imported infection.

The sex ratio is part of routine care too. Keep one male to two or three females, or else a group of four or more males
where aggression gets spread around. Two males alone is the worst option: the dominant one will harass the other
non-stop.

## Compatibility

Swordtails are peaceful by temperament and make excellent community fish. The only caveat is rivalry between males,
solved by the right sex ratio and enough volume.

Good tank mates:

- [platy](/en/xiphophorus-maculatus) and [variatus platy](/en/xiphophorus-variatus) — close relatives with the same
  water requirements;
- [molly](/en/poecilia-sphenops) and [guppy](/en/poecilia-reticulata) — the same hard, alkaline water;
- [zebra danio](/en/danio-rerio) — just as active and just as hardy;
- [bronze corydoras](/en/corydoras-aeneus) — a calm catfish for the bottom level;
- [bristlenose pleco](/en/ancistrus-dolichopterus) — an algae eater that competes for no space.

Poor choices: aggressive cichlids and any fin-nipper, which will go for the male's sword first. Large
[angelfish](/en/pterophyllum-scalare) get along with adult swordtails but will certainly eat the fry. Soft-water
characins such as the [neon tetra](/en/paracheirodon-innesi) are compatible in temperament yet need acidic, soft water —
any compromise on parameters would shortchange both species.

One important warning: swordtails interbreed freely with platies and produce fertile hybrids. If you want to keep your
lines pure, do not house the two species together.

## Breeding

Breeding swordtails is easier than preventing it: this is a livebearer, and in a community tank offspring simply appear
without any input from the aquarist.

The female carries her brood for 4-6 weeks and then gives birth to 20-80 fully formed fry that swim and feed
immediately. Signs that birth is near are a swollen, squared-off belly and a darkened gravid spot near the anal fin. One
extremely useful trait: a female can store sperm and deliver several more broods without mating again.

If your goal is to save as many fry as possible, move the female to a separate 10-20 litre container thick with java
moss or hornwort and return her to the main tank right after she gives birth. Breeding traps do work, but the cramped
space and stress often trigger premature birth — use them only briefly and keep an eye on the female. In a community
tank without intervention 5-10% of fry survive; dense planting and floating plants raise that share considerably.

Feed the fry four to five times a day on crushed flakes, baby brine shrimp and microworms. With generous feeding and
regular water changes they grow fast: sex becomes distinguishable at 2-3 months and maturity arrives at 3-5 months. The
male's sword develops gradually and only reaches full length towards the end of the first year.

One practical tip: do not raise a whole brood in a single grow-out tank. Overcrowding during the growth stage is the
main reason beautiful parents produce small, drab offspring.
