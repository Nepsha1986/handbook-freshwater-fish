## Overview

_Nimbochromis venustus_ is a large member of the Lake Malawi haplochromine group, living over sandy and intermediate
zones at depths of 10-30 metres. It is one of the few aquarium species with a genuinely specialised hunting technique:
the Venustus half-buries itself in the sand and freezes, its blotched pattern rendering it almost invisible against the
bottom. Any small fish that comes too close disappears in a single lunge.

Adults reach 20-25 cm and live 8-10 years. Juveniles and females share the same colouration: a silvery-yellow ground
with large irregular brown blotches genuinely reminiscent of a giraffe's hide. With age the males change dress
completely — the head turns bright blue, the forehead and gill covers take on a turquoise sheen, a yellow stripe runs
along the back and the blotches all but vanish. The transformation begins at around 12-15 cm and stretches over several
months, which is why juveniles are frequently sold as females.

In temperament this is a semi-aggressive, territorial fish. The Venustus lacks the sheer belligerence of the Malawi
mbuna, but it is large, powerful and regards anything smaller than itself as food. Care level is intermediate: the water
requirements are standard for Malawi, and the real difficulty is the volume required.

## Tank Requirements

The minimum is 300 litres at 150 cm or longer, and that really is a minimum for a group of one male and three or four
females. Attempting to keep a Venustus in 200 litres is pointless: the fish grows large and needs room to move.

Water parameters: temperature 24-28 °C, pH 7.5-8.5, general hardness (GH) 10-20 dGH. Malawi species need a stable
alkaline environment, and gradual acidification of the water is the most common cause of health problems.

The layout must combine two elements. The first is a sand bed 5-7 cm deep: this is what the fish buries itself in, and
without sand the hunting behaviour simply never appears — fine gravel additionally damages the gill covers. The second
is rockwork along the back and side walls, providing shelter and dividing the space into territories. Leave the centre
of the tank open.

Plants are essentially futile here, as the fish digs them up. If you want greenery, limit yourself to anubias and
vallisneria secured firmly to the rocks.

Filtration must be powerful — a large predator imposes a heavy biological load. Aim for six to eight tank volumes per
hour. Crushed coral in the filter helps hold alkalinity. A lid is mandatory.

## Feeding and Diet

In nature _N. venustus_ is an ambush piscivore feeding on small fish and fry. Feeding it live fish in the aquarium,
however, is both unnecessary and dangerous: it is a direct route to introducing parasites and ich.

The staple should be quality pellets for large carnivorous cichlids at 40-45% protein. Supplement with frozen foods:
mysis, krill, brine shrimp, pieces of prawn, white fish fillet. Offer bloodworms in moderation.

Exclude tubifex, beef heart and any food made from warm-blooded animals entirely. Malawi cichlids are highly prone to
Malawi bloat, a disorder of the gut, and fatty protein-rich food provokes it almost reliably. For the same reason a
regular spirulina-based supplement is beneficial.

Feed adults once or twice a day with one fasting day per week. A portion should be consumed within two to three minutes.

## Care and Maintenance

A weekly 25-30% water change is mandatory: a large fish on a protein diet fouls the tank quickly. Prepare the water in
advance, matching temperature, hardness and pH.

Watch the nitrate and keep it below 25 mg/l. It is nitrate accumulation combined with excess protein that triggers
Malawi bloat, whose first signs are refusal of food, darkening colour and whitish stringy faeces, followed by a swollen
abdomen. Caught early it responds to metronidazole; advanced cases almost always end in death.

Vacuum the sand weekly in open areas. Rinse filter media in old tank water every three to four weeks, never more than
half at a time. Check pH weekly.

A healthy Venustus is active, brightly coloured and holds station in the open part of the tank. Check the stability of
the rockwork too: a fish this size can shift stones by undermining the substrate.

## Compatibility

_N. venustus_ is semi-aggressive and predatory, so choosing tank mates comes down to a simple rule: they must be large
enough not to fit in its mouth and calm enough not to provoke conflict.

Suitable companions are large Malawi haplochromines of similar size and temperament:
[Electric Blue Hap](/en/sciaenochromis-fryeri), [Red Empress](/en/protomelas-taeniolatus),
[Red Fin Borleyi](/en/copadichromis-borleyi) and [Aulonocara nyassae](/en/aulonocara-nyassae). Among bottom dwellers the
[Cuckoo Catfish](/en/synodontis-multipunctatus) fits well.

Categorically unsuitable: any fish under 8-10 cm, which will be eaten — small mbuna and a juvenile
[Yellow Lab Cichlid](/en/labidochromis-caeruleus) included; aggressive mbuna such as the
[Auratus Cichlid](/en/melanochromis-auratus), which will provoke constant fighting; and all South American and Asian
species needing soft, slightly acidic water.

Within the species the standard Malawi scheme applies: one male to three or four females. Two adult males in one tank
will almost inevitably end with the weaker one beaten to death.

## Breeding

_N. venustus_ is a maternal mouthbrooder, and with the right group and volume it breeds without any particular effort.
No dedicated breeding tank is required.

Keep one male and three or four females: the ratio matters, because a spawning male pursues females persistently, and in
a pair a single fish absorbs all that attention. Sexual maturity comes at one and a half to two years at around 15 cm,
coinciding with the male's colour change.

Conditions: temperature 26-28 °C, pH 8.0-8.5, stable parameters and generous feeding. The male clears a patch of sand
and displays to the female. She lays 50-120 eggs and takes them into her mouth immediately; fertilisation happens there.

Mouthbrooding lasts 18-21 days, during which the female does not feed and visibly loses condition. She can be left in
the main tank if it is spacious and offers shelter, but it is safer to move her gently into a separate 50-70 litre
container filled with water from the main tank — stressed females often spit out or swallow the clutch.

Released fry take brine shrimp nauplii and crushed pellets straight away. For the first few days the female still
shelters them in her mouth when alarmed; after that the bond ends and she is returned to the main tank. The young grow
fast and need frequent water changes and moderate feeding to stay healthy.
