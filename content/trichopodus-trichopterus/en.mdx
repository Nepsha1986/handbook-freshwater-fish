## Overview

_Trichopodus trichopterus_ is a freshwater labyrinth fish from the family Osphronemidae and one of the most widespread
species in the aquarium hobby. In the wild it inhabits slow-moving rivers, canals, swamps, rice paddies, and flooded
lowlands of Southeast Asia: the Mekong basin, Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, as well as the islands of
Sumatra, Java, and Borneo. Thanks to its exceptional hardiness, the fish has spread far beyond its natural range.

The common name refers to the two dark spots on the silvery-blue body: one in the middle of the flank, the second at the
base of the caudal fin. The eye is traditionally counted as the third "spot". Adults reach 10–15 cm; females are
noticeably fuller-bodied, while the male's dorsal fin is longer and pointed. With proper care, _Trichopodus
trichopterus_ lives 4–6 years.

Decades of selective breeding have produced numerous color forms: the blue gourami (var. sumatranus), the marbled or
opaline form (also known as the Cosby gourami), gold, silver, and platinum. All belong to the same species and differ
only in coloration. Like other labyrinth fish, the three-spot gourami breathes atmospheric air through its labyrinth
organ, which allows it to survive in waters with extremely low oxygen levels. The thread-like ventral fins serve as
tactile organs — the fish constantly uses them to explore its surroundings.

## Tank Requirements

A group of one male and two to three females requires an aquarium of at least 120 liters. Optimal water parameters:
temperature 22–28 °C, pH 6.0–8.0, general hardness (GH) 5–20. The species tolerates fluctuations calmly, which is what
makes it such a good choice for beginners, though sudden swings are still best avoided.

Filtration should be effective but without strong current — in nature these fish stay in still and slow-moving water.
Furnish the tank with dense plantings (vallisneria, cryptocorynes, anubias), driftwood, and, importantly, floating
plants such as water lettuce, riccia, or salvinia. Floating plants diffuse the light and create surface cover where the
male later builds his bubble nest. A dark substrate makes the coloration more contrasting.

Important: cover the aquarium with a lid, leaving an air gap between the water surface and the cover glass. Labyrinth
fish gulp air from the surface, and cold room air can inflame the labyrinth organ.

## Feeding and Diet

_Trichopodus trichopterus_ is omnivorous and entirely unfussy about food. The staple diet consists of quality dry foods
— flakes or small pellets. To maintain health and vivid coloration, supplement the menu regularly with live and frozen
foods: bloodworms, brine shrimp, daphnia, and glassworms. Vegetable matter is beneficial too — blanched spinach,
zucchini, or spirulina-based foods.

Feed once or twice a day in small portions: the fish is prone to overeating and puts on fat easily with age. One useful
habit deserves a separate mention — three-spot gouramis readily eat hydra and planaria, so they are sometimes added to a
tank specifically to control these pests.

## Care and Maintenance

This is one of the most undemanding aquarium fish and can be safely recommended to beginners. A weekly water change of
20–30 % and regular substrate cleaning are enough. There are no special equipment requirements; a heater is needed only
in cool rooms.

Make sure at least part of the water surface stays clear: the fish needs constant access to air. Drafts and a sharp
difference between water and air temperature are dangerous — they are the most common cause of labyrinth problems. Under
proper conditions the species rarely falls ill; typical issues stem from poor water quality and overfeeding.

Males grow increasingly territorial with age, so the tank should offer plenty of shelters and visual barriers of plants
that let weaker individuals stay out of the dominant fish's sight.

## Compatibility

Young three-spot gouramis are peaceful, but adult males become territorial toward their own kind, other gouramis, and
their own females. The best arrangement is one male with two or three females: the male's attention is then spread among
several fish and none is harassed continuously.

Good tank mates include [harlequin rasboras](/en/trigonostigma-heteromorpha), [zebra danios](/en/danio-rerio),
[glowlight tetras](/en/hemigrammus-erythrozonus), [corydoras](/en/corydoras-aeneus), and
[bristlenose plecos](/en/ancistrus-cirrhosus) — active but non-aggressive fish of comparable or smaller size that occupy
other levels of the tank.

Avoid keeping the three-spot gourami with [bettas](/en/betta-splendens) or with smaller, shyer labyrinth fish such as
[dwarf gouramis](/en/trichogaster-lalius) and [honey gouramis](/en/trichogaster-chuna) — they will be bullied.
[Tiger barbs](/en/puntius-tetrazona) and other fin-nippers will shred the gourami's thread-like ventral fins. Small
shrimp and fry are seen by an adult fish purely as food.

## Breeding

Breeding the three-spot gourami is straightforward — it is one of the best species for a first attempt at spawning
labyrinth fish. You will need a separate breeding tank of 40–60 liters with a water level of 15–20 cm, a temperature of
27–29 °C, soft slightly acidic water, and floating plants. Current in the breeding tank must be minimal, otherwise the
nest will break apart.

Condition the breeders for one to two weeks beforehand with generous feedings of live food. The male builds a bubble
nest at the surface, then begins courtship and "embraces" the female, helping her release the eggs. A single spawning
yields 500 to 2000 eggs; the buoyant eggs float up and the male collects them into the nest.

Remove the female immediately after spawning — the male will chase her. The father guards and repairs the nest until the
larvae hatch in 24–36 hours. Once the fry become free-swimming after three to four days, remove the male as well. Start
the fry on infusoria or liquid fry food, switching to baby brine shrimp a few days later. While the labyrinth organ
forms (at roughly three to four weeks of age), keep the breeding tank tightly covered: warm, humid air above the water
is critical for the fry to develop properly.
